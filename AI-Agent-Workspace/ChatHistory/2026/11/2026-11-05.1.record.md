```text
Written for you, as the person deciding the Explorer's shape, at the point where the gesture is changed but the composition is still open.

## What I want from this pass

One sentence of behaviour: the picture moves only when you meant it to. Everything else follows from that, and I kept the pass to the gesture on purpose.

- **The gesture should match the cost.** Opening a description grows the card and re-solves the layout around it. That is an expensive, visible change, so it has to be bought with a deliberate act, a tap or Enter on the name, never with the pointer passing by on its way to a button. Hover now costs nothing: it lights the row's wires and draws no text.
- **Less machinery, not more.** The hover design needed a return point, a hold override, a camera target, a pause of the layout search and an intent timer to make an involuntary gesture tolerable. Each existed to repair a consequence of the wrong trigger. With the trigger changed, I deleted them rather than switching them off. The source diff is net negative:

| Changed source | Lines added | Lines removed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explorer client and specs | 261 | 412 |

- **Verification that tells the truth about the new design.** The reading spec now asserts what a tap promises: the name under your hand stays put, the same set of open descriptions gives the same picture, and nothing moves on hover. One assertion I had carried over from the hover era, that a fold returns every neighbour to its exact prior place, turned out not to be a promise the tap design makes. I replaced the assertion instead of re-adding the home-restore machinery to satisfy it. More on that below, because you will feel it.

## The theory, in five rules

- **Attention is not intent.** Where the pointer rests tells the Explorer what you are looking at. It may acknowledge that with lit wires, but it must not re-solve the drawing. Hover is continuous and involuntary: reaching the More button means crossing three names, and if crossing a name rearranges the map, reaching the button becomes a chase. This is your own rule from September, a click pins and a hover peeks, and your March refusal of a hover that re-renders. I am applying it, not inventing it.
- **The thing you touched is the fixed point.** When a card grows, the name you tapped must not move; the growth happens below it. The pass had to earn this twice. Holding the tapped row let the layout seat the taller card higher, and under the hover anchor tuning the re-rendered row under the resting pointer re-anchored the hold mid-move. Both are fixed, and the name now moves by less than a thousandth of a pixel on a tap.

| Defect found in this pass | Name leap | State |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Row held instead of the card's name | 103 px | fixed |
| Hover re-anchoring during the tap's move | 272 px | fixed |

- **One meaning per control.** Before, a name hovered previewed, clicked opened the reader, keyboard focus previewed, and the book did a third thing. Now a name opens or folds its own text, by tap or Enter, and reports that state to assistive technology; the book is a second way to the same thing. The evidence reader is reached from More, from the Read full description link inside an opened block, from search and from the pin tray. The common act, reading the authored text in place, gets the cheapest gesture; the deeper act, evidence, gets one more step. I made that trade because you said the Detail Panel is becoming less useful by the day, and because the link at the end of an opened description is the natural door to the evidence: read first, go deeper from inside the text.
- **Reading is state, not a mode.** Taps accumulate: open PaymentService, open Post, fold PaymentService. Nothing you do elsewhere silently cancels what you opened; a pin or a pan leaves it alone. Only the scale rule folds prose, and Reset Zoom restores it. Because what is open is saved in the URL, a link you send carries what you were reading. That is the Explorer's own ethos applied to reading: the view is a reproducible fact.
- **Fade the irrelevant; do not move it.** Where the design wants to direct attention, it should dim what is not relevant rather than relocate what is. Growth on a tap is the one sanctioned move, and I want it to stay the only one.

## What I measured, and your reserved-space picture

I found a behaviour I do not like and did not change. At the overview scale a tap does more than open one description: the card switches from its compact drawing to its reading drawing, with larger type and the Uses and Used by controls, and the layout re-solves around a card twice the height. Measured on the payment service at 900 by 700 with the idle search off:

| Step | Card size at 0.83 scale | Left column order |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the tap | 309 × 162 px | Query, Result, Request |
| Open | 433 × 337 px | Request, Result, Query |
| Fold | 309 × 162 px | Request, Result, Query |

The fold restores the card but not the order, because the solve begins from the picture the open made and keeps what it finds. A fresh load with the same folded entry draws the original order, so this is the solve's history, not the saved reading. The pin specs promise the same shape of thing, the acted-on row still and the rest sliding to new places, and none promises that unpinning restores the old picture. This is the layout engine's competition, not the reading gesture, which is why I left it. But it is exactly the cost that makes reading feel expensive, and I think it is the next thing to look at: a stability preference in the competition so the current arrangement wins unless the gain is clear, and no drawing switch on a tap at overview scale, so one block opens inside the compact drawing.

Your original picture, an even slot per symbol with an ellipsis and a click to the reader, is the strongest possible form of "nothing moves when you read", and I take it seriously. Its cost is paid always rather than when you read: every documented card is tall whether or not anyone is reading it, and on a map of eleven cards that is a lot of height spent on text nobody is looking at. The ellipsis also makes the slot a teaser. You still click, and what you click into is the Detail Panel you are losing faith in.

My instinct is a middle composition: one line of gist per symbol always visible, the first sentence of its description cut with an ellipsis, and a tap unfolding the full text in place. The card is a little taller always, one line per documented symbol, and when you do read, the card still grows, but the gist already told you whether it was worth it. There is a fourth candidate that also fits the theory: a hover popover that floats over the map outside the layout, so it moves nothing, with a tap to keep it. It is the classic answer to "a hover peeks" and has the classic problems: it occludes neighbours and wires, touch has no hover, and it flickers when crossing rows.

You asked whether we mocked up enough ways to draw these cards. I do not think we did. The honest next step is a matched comparison at the same scale, subject and pins: the current tap-to-grow, your reserved slot, the gist line, and the popover, rendered for real and judged by your eye. None of them is built in this pass.

On Astra's work: the reading surface, the state model, shared reading in the URL and the Uses and Used by menus are good, and this pass stands on them. Where it went wrong is instructive. A return point, a hold override, a camera target, a search pause and an intent timer are each reasonable alone, and together they are the signature of a gesture fighting the layout. When a design needs that much compensation, the trigger is wrong, not the compensations.

The full gate is still running. When it reports I will finish the record, write the direction outcome and commit.
```

```text
You are right, and the facts underneath make the case stronger than the overview picture alone.

## What the drawing switch actually is

It is one CSS class, `local-reading-card`, applied to a card whenever any of its descriptions is visible, at every scale, not only the overview. It was built on November 3 and 4 so that a card being read is legible without zooming, like a page. When it fires, the card changes type scale and composition at once:

| Part of the card | Ordinary drawing | While any description shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symbol names | 11 px | 16 px |
| File name | card title size | 22 px |
| Description prose | not drawn | 15 px, clamped at 280 px with a Read full description link |
| Uses and Used by row | hidden | shown |
| Directory line | shown | hidden |

So a tap today does two things: it discloses one description, and it re-composes the whole card at a larger scale. The held name hides the second effect at reading scale, because the card re-composes around a still name. At the overview scale nothing hides it, which is the 2× growth and the reordered column you saw.

## Your proposal, restated as rules

I think your instinct is right, and I would state it as a rule rather than a tweak: **a card's box depends only on the file's structure, never on zoom and never on what is being read.** Concretely:

- One type scale per card, chosen to be legible at the calibrated reading scale. The reading class and its 16 px and 22 px go.
- A fixed slot per described symbol, two lines, and a two-line slot for the file's Purpose. Undocumented symbols keep a single row. Text that does not fit is cut with an ellipsis.
- A tap on a name opens the full text in the reader dialog, which is the surface you named in your earlier message. Nothing opens inside the layout, so the book control and the open and fold choices lose their reason to exist.
- Scale changes what is drawn inside the box, not the box. Prose fades as you zoom out instead of folding; the fold and restore ratios and the reveal exceptions go with it.
- Layout shifts then happen only on structural acts: pins, opening a directory, Centre.

That is a real simplification of the reading module, not just a nicer picture.

## What it costs, honestly

**Height, always.** Estimated for the payment service at one-to-one scale, with the two-line slots and today's line height:

| Card | Height today, folded | Height today, read | Height with slots, always |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PaymentService.cs, Purpose and 3 described symbols | about 195 px | about 405 px | about 360 px, estimate |

So the slot card is roughly as tall as today's read card, all the time. The existing cap that hides rows beyond a few, the "+N symbols" line, bounds the worst case, and a card with no descriptions does not grow at all. Your "very very very tall" worry is real for a card with seven documented symbols, and the cap is the answer to it, or a one-line slot for symbols beyond the first few.

**Width, fixed.** Today the folded card is narrower than the read card because prose asks for about fifty characters a line. A constant box means choosing one width. I would choose the reading width, so most estate descriptions show whole in two lines, which makes every documented card wider than today's folded card and pushes the overview one step further out. That is a density change you should judge by eye.

**Legibility at small scale.** With one type scale, reading means zooming to the card, as on a map. At the overview the slots are faint text. The reading-class design avoided that by enlarging the card instead, which is exactly the shift you want gone, so this is the trade.

**What we lose.** The page-like card, where a selected file reads like a document inside the map. The reader dialog covers that need.

Commit today's pass as it stands once the gate is green: the gesture rule and the deleted hover machinery are right under either composition, and the verified baseline is worth having.
```
